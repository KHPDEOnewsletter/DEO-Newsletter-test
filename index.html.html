PhD Spotlight

Queen Anoma – Culturally Tailored Weight Management Research

Queen Anoma is a PhD candidate whose research focuses on culturally tailored weight management interventions for people of Black heritage in south London. With a background in nursing, she combines clinical experience with public health research to address inequalities in obesity care.

Her work explores how culturally adapted digital interventions can improve engagement, retention, and outcomes for individuals living with complex obesity. She has recently completed a systematic review and meta-analysis examining the effectiveness and acceptability of behavioural interventions among ethnic minority populations.

The next phase of her research will involve qualitative work and co-design with Black African communities in south London to develop an inclusive, specialist weight management intervention. Alongside her PhD, Queen works as a specialist weight management nurse at Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust, supporting the rollout of GLP-1 therapies and contributing to more inclusive, person-centred care pathways.

Mothers and babies at growing risk without better care for type 2 diabetes

Researchers from across the UK and Ireland are calling for urgent action to improve care for women with early-onset type 2 diabetes before, during, and after pregnancy.

The call follows a new consensus statement developed by clinicians and researchers, including Sara White from King's College London. Published in Diabetic Medicine, the statement brings together evidence from three systematic reviews and discussions from the Diabetes UK Annual Professional Conference in 2025, highlighting critical gaps in care and research.

The authors emphasise that type 2 diabetes in women of reproductive age is rising, yet evidence to guide care remains limited. Key priorities include improving preconception care, strengthening postnatal follow-up, and addressing inequalities affecting outcomes. The statement calls on funders, policymakers and healthcare leaders to act, warning that without targeted investment, preventable harms to mothers and babies will continue to increase.

Hybrid closed-loop systems in people with type 1 diabetes and comorbid mental health conditions

Congratulations to the King's College Hospital NHS FT diabetes team on the publication of their paper in Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism (January 2026). The study examined clinical outcomes of adults with type 1 diabetes and comorbid depression and/or anxiety disorders using hybrid closed-loop technology.

The findings highlight the benefits of a service model incorporating mental health professionals within the multidisciplinary diabetes team, and demonstrate the value of a robust audit culture and contributions of medical students engaged in clinical research activities.

A weight management single point of access (SPOA) for South East London

Following the weight management pathway work carried out last year, one of the key recommendations was to implement a SPOA in order to improve and streamline access to weight management services in south east London. Transforming Adult Weight Management Services in South East London — Report and Recommendations (July 2025)

A working group of stakeholders, which involves partners from across the system and residents with lived experience, has been convened to take this recommendation forward. An initial workshop was held in January 2026, providing an opportunity for stakeholders to review a high-level single-point-of-access pathway, breaking it into sections for small-group discussions.

The groups focused on digital functionality, workforce, outcome measures, and ensuring equity across services. Conversations also centred around ensuring mental health was a key element in any assessment/triage process and centring patient choice within decision making.

The next stage, working with the working group, is to develop a service specification for a Single Point of Access for weight management services in south east London based on our work to date, and to seek to implement a service.

Promoting Inclusive Research: KHP DEO × Mabadiliko

Inclusive research is central to the DEO Research Framework (2025–2030) and our ambition to deliver equitable health outcomes for diverse populations across south east London.

Last year, we partnered with Mabadiliko to undertake community-led insight work with south east London adults. A four-stage co-production approach (literature review, survey, focus groups, and co-design workshop) identified and prioritised high-impact, feasible interventions for implementation within DEO.

Community-led insight identified three priority needs:

  • Understanding the unique needs of diverse communities;
  • Making research more accessible and inclusive;
  • Tools and support for meaningful involvement.

This co-production work surfaced two priority interventions:

  1. A Community Engagement Protocol to standardise equitable engagement across DEO research
  2. An Accessible Research Design Toolkit to embed inclusion at the proposal and study design stage

These insights are now guiding our approach to delivering the inclusion commitment set out in the DEO Research Framework. These tools will be co-designed, tested and refined in partnership with communities and VCSE organisations.

Metabolic Associations of Major Mental Illness Workshop

On 18 March 2026, the KHP DEO Programme convened Crossing the Divide: Metabolic Health in Major Mental Illness, marking the first meeting of this Research Focus Area Group.

Chaired by Toby Pillinger and Marietta Stadler, the workshop brought together clinicians, researchers, students, funders and industry to examine how metabolic health must be integrated into mental health research and care. Across keynote presentations, research findings and funder discussions, a consistent message emerged: closing the 15–20 year mortality gap in severe mental illness (SMI) requires a step-change in how we conceptualise illness, design services, and deliver interdisciplinary research.

The keynote from Dr Shubulade Smith CBE (President of the Royal College of Psychiatrists and Consultant Psychiatrist at South London and Maudsley NHS Foundation Trust) reinforced a critical shift: SMI should be understood as a whole-body condition, with metabolic dysfunction present from early stages and requiring integrated approaches across care, research and commissioning.

The workshop included:

  • A keynote address on integrating metabolic and mental health research
  • A funder panel discussion with Breakthrough T1D, Diabetes UK, Bipolar UK and Novo Nordisk
  • Research spotlights spanning early-career to senior researchers, covering:
    • Type 1 diabetes technology and mental health — Adam Shooshtarian & Jonathan Croos, King's College London
    • Treatment of binge eating disorder — Dr Aisling McCarthy, University College Dublin
    • Co-produced research priorities for metabolic interventions in SMI — Dr Dina Farran, IoPPN, King's College London
    • A dose–response analysis of GLP-1 receptor agonists and digital tool development — Dr Ellice Caldwell Dunn, IoPPN, King's College London
  • Informal networking to support new collaborations

Key themes from the workshop

Across the workshop, several consistent themes emerged shaping future research and practice:

  • Metabolic health must be embedded as core clinical care, not treated as secondary to mental health;
  • Risk begins early, with metabolic dysfunction present from illness onset;
  • Integrated approaches are required, addressing behavioural, biological and treatment-related drivers together;
  • Inclusive and meaningful PPIE is essential, but must go beyond single engagements using multiple methods, ensuring diversity, and actively reaching underserved populations;
  • The major gap is implementation, not evidence, with existing integrated care models not consistently commissioned or scaled;
  • Emerging therapies (e.g. GLP-1 receptor agonists) present new opportunities but require robust, standardised evaluation.

A further key theme was the need for standardised, meaningful outcome frameworks that capture both mental and physical health, enabling consistency across studies and supporting real-world implementation.

What makes a strong proposal in this space?

Across the funder panel, several consistent signals emerged:

  • Clear interdisciplinary approaches (psychiatry, metabolic health and population health);
  • Strong, embedded PPIE from the outset, with diverse representation and community engagement;
  • Focus on unmet need and health inequalities;
  • Evidence of implementation potential and real-world impact;
  • Alignment with system priorities (e.g. NHS, policy and global health).

A key challenge highlighted was the difficulty of engaging underserved populations, reinforcing the need for targeted, community-based approaches and long-term partnerships rather than passive recruitment.

Funders also emphasised the importance of: cross-checking PPIE insights beyond small groups; using mixed engagement methods (e.g. surveys, repeated engagement); and designing research that can drive measurable service change.

Workshop Impact

The workshop met expectations for 93% of respondents (59% response rate). Participants identified clear actions they intend to take, including:

  • Applying learning within MDT discussions and clinical practice;
  • Strengthening collaboration between mental and physical health teams;
  • Exploring new research, project, and funding opportunities;
  • Reflecting differently on their clinical roles (e.g., dietetics, metabolic care);
  • Raising awareness of DEO work and considering service developments (e.g. metabolic clinics).
A field ready for change. The link between metabolic and mental health is no longer conceptual — it is a clinical and research priority. For clinicians, this means embedding metabolic care into routine mental health practice. For researchers, it requires designing studies that are interdisciplinary, inclusive and implementation-focused. For students, it signals a future where cross-disciplinary thinking is essential. For funders, it means prioritising proposals that demonstrate real-world impact, tackle inequalities and support scalable system change.

Upcoming Training & Events

Equity in Diabetes & Pregnancy Event — Thursday 30 April 2026

A recent event hosted by Diabetes Africa brought together healthcare professionals, community leaders and people with lived experience to explore how diabetes care can become more equitable, culturally responsive and community centred.

Discussions focused on improving pregnancy preparation and outcomes for women living with diabetes, particularly Black and Asian women who continue to face significant inequalities. Speakers highlighted the importance of ongoing support before, during and after pregnancy, noting that 1 in 2 women with gestational diabetes will go on to develop type 2 diabetes.

Lived experience shared during the day reinforced gaps in postnatal follow-up and the need for more joined-up, multidisciplinary care. Attendees also emphasised that "every contact counts", with a role for professionals across primary care, maternity and pharmacy services.

The importance of cultural humility was a key theme, recognising the diversity within communities and the need for tailored, meaningful conversations. The event closed with a clear call for continued collaboration, education and community partnership to drive more equitable care.
